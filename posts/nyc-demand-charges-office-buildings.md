# Demand charges in NYC office buildings and what an ice battery can do

New York City is a strong market for thermal storage because large commercial buildings do not just pay for energy. They also pay for peak power.

For many large commercial customers, one hot afternoon can create a billing problem that lasts all month. If cooling drives the building's highest 15-minute interval, that single spike can set a meaningful share of the bill.

This post walks through a practical office-building case study, uses typical NYC-style numbers, and compares them with real examples from the market.

## Why demand charges matter so much in NYC

Con Edison separates large commercial customers into rate classes where demand matters. Its rate education materials explicitly distinguish smaller commercial service from larger customers whose bills include demand charges. In parallel, Con Edison explains in its billing FAQ that commercial and industrial customers pay demand charges because they require more expensive grid equipment and because the utility must stand ready to serve their highest usage interval.

NYSERDA's RTEM demand-management training material makes the point even more directly: for a large commercial electricity customer in New York City, demand delivery charges can account for roughly 30% to 70% of the total monthly electricity cost, and can routinely exceed the energy charge itself.

That is exactly why cooling matters. Cooling is one of the largest flexible electric loads in an office tower, and it tends to peak at the same time the city peaks.

## A simple NYC office case study

Assume a Manhattan office building with these characteristics:

- 100,000 square feet
- Existing electric chiller plant serving summer cooling
- Summer peak cooling load around 450 kW
- Annual cooling energy around 720,000 kWh
- Effective demand charge around $30/kW-month during the cooling season
- Electricity price around $0.18/kWh blended for cooling energy
- Six meaningful cooling months per year

These are not official tariff values for every property. They are illustrative numbers chosen to match the economics that many NYC office owners already recognize: high summer demand charges and expensive daytime cooling.

Under that setup:

- Baseline annual cooling-related demand charges are about `450 kW x $30/kW x 6 months = $81,000`
- Baseline annual cooling energy charges are about `720,000 kWh x $0.18/kWh = $129,600`
- Total annual cooling bill is about `$210,600`

That means demand charges alone are about 38% of this simplified cooling bill. That lands squarely inside the range NYSERDA highlights for large New York City commercial customers.

## What happens if an ice battery shifts the peak

Now assume an ice battery shifts 40% of the building's peak cooling load out of the expensive interval.

- Peak load shifted: `450 kW x 40% = 180 kW`
- Annual demand-charge savings: `180 kW x $30/kW x 6 months = $32,400`

If the system also shifts roughly 230,000 kWh of annual cooling energy to lower-cost nighttime operation, and if that shifted energy is effectively about 35% cheaper, then:

- Annual energy-charge savings are about `230,000 kWh x $0.18/kWh x 35% = $14,490`

Total modeled annual savings are then about:

- `$32,400 + $14,490 = $46,890`

That is about 22% of the simplified annual cooling bill.

The important takeaway is not the exact second decimal place. The takeaway is the split:

- Most of the value comes from lowering the hottest-hour kW spike
- A smaller but still real share comes from moving cooling work to nighttime

In NYC, that is the right economic intuition.

## Why this lines up with real market behavior

This is not just a spreadsheet story.

CALMAC's overview of commercial electricity bills says demand is often 40% to 60% of the total bill for North American commercial and industrial customers. Its cooling-cost overview says demand charges can be 20% to 80% of monthly energy costs and notes that in many systems only a portion of cooling load needs to be shifted to materially lower the bill.

NYSERDA's training material is even more relevant because it is New York-specific. Its demand-management resource states that demand delivery charges for a large commercial electricity customer in New York City can be 30% to 70% of the monthly electricity bill. That validates the central claim behind Icebreaker: in NYC, peak kW can be as important as total kWh.

## Existing examples that support the thesis

### Rockefeller Center

Rockefeller Center is one of the clearest proof points.

According to CALMAC's case study:

- The central plant serves roughly 8 million rentable square feet across ten buildings
- The site has 14,500 tons of steam and electric chillers
- The ice system was sized to provide 8,600 ton-hours of thermal storage
- The project provided 1,025 kW of thermal energy storage

The operating logic is exactly what you would expect in NYC. During peak-demand periods, cooling from the ice storage tanks is used instead of running another electric chiller. The case study quotes Tishman Speyer's Joseph Szabo saying that turning on large chillers can result in large electrical demand cost penalties, especially in New York City with its high electrical-demand utility rate structure.

The most telling operational result is simple: before storage, the plant would run one 4,000-ton steam turbine chiller, one 4,000-ton electric chiller, and one 2,500-ton electric chiller on peak cooling days. After incorporating the ice burn, the operators were able to avoid bringing the 2,500-ton electric chiller online.

That is exactly the game: avoid one more large daytime machine during the interval that sets the bill.

### New Water Street

NYSERDA's New Water Street ice-storage case study describes a downtown Manhattan building owner choosing ice thermal energy storage specifically to reduce operating costs by lowering demand charges while also supporting sustainability goals.

Even without reproducing every equipment detail here, the strategic point is important: sophisticated NYC property owners have already used ice storage not as a science project, but as a demand-charge-management tool.

### RTEM examples from NYC owners

NYSERDA's RTEM success-story library includes Edison Properties, SL Green, Rudin, and Jack Resnick. Those are not all thermal-storage examples, but they matter because they show the same underlying truth: experienced NYC owners spend real money and management attention reducing peak demand and tightening building operations.

Thermal storage fits into that same operating stack. It is not competing with better controls. It is complementary to them.

## Where an ice battery works best

The economics are strongest when a building has:

- High summer afternoon cooling peaks
- Existing chilled-water or central plant infrastructure
- Large enough load for demand charges to matter
- Nighttime operating flexibility for charging storage
- An owner who wants bill savings first, and grid participation later

The economics are weaker when:

- Cooling loads are already flat and modest
- The building has little demand-charge exposure
- The HVAC system is too fragmented to retrofit cleanly

## The strategic value beyond the first savings case

The first sale is usually a building economics sale: lower demand charges, some off-peak energy savings, less need to run the biggest equipment during the worst hours.

But the longer-term strategic value is portfolio coordination. Once multiple buildings can pre-charge at night and discharge through hot afternoon peaks, a landlord or operator has the beginning of a thermal virtual power plant.

That matters in a city where grid strain and cooling demand increasingly line up.

## Bottom line

For large NYC office buildings, demand charges are not a side issue. They are often one of the main reasons cooling is expensive.

A simple office-building case with a 450 kW cooling peak shows why: shifting about 180 kW of that peak can create roughly $32,000 per year in demand-charge savings before counting the added benefit of cheaper nighttime energy. Add the energy shift and total annual cooling-bill savings can approach $47,000 in a 100,000-square-foot example.

That logic is consistent with what the market has already shown:

- NYSERDA documents how large NYC bills can be dominated by demand charges
- CALMAC and Trane describe thermal storage as a way to avoid peak-demand penalties
- Rockefeller Center is a concrete New York example of operators using stored cooling to avoid running another large electric chiller during peak hours

For a city full of office buildings with expensive cooling peaks, ice batteries are not exotic. They are a practical way to buy less of the wrong electricity at the wrong time.
