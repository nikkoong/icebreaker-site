I am a mechanical and thermal engineer who has spent time thinking about heat in a lot of different contexts: defense-grade cooling systems, the hottest sections of jet engines, and the awkward reality that some of the most important infrastructure in modern life is hidden in ceilings, plant rooms, and utility bills that few people want to read.

For a while, that interest in thermal systems ran in parallel with another part of my career. I worked as a product leader building AI systems at scale for more than 150 million customers. That experience taught me how to think about systems, incentives, operations, and bottlenecks. It also made one thing very clear to me: the most interesting problems are often the ones hiding in plain sight.

Cooling is one of those problems.

![Founder of Icebreaker Energy](../nikko_profile_pic.jpg)

I started thinking more seriously about it after living through a muggy New York City summer and noticing something hard to ignore. Our buildings were spending enormous amounts of electricity to reject heat back into the city, right when the grid was already strained and electricity was most expensive. Comfort depended on cooling, but the way we delivered it often felt crude: make cold instantly, at the hottest and most expensive moment, and then act surprised by the bill.

That did not seem like a law of physics. It seemed like a systems-design failure.

Icebreaker Energy comes from the belief that cooling should be smarter. Buildings should be able to make cold when the grid is under less strain, store it, and use it later. That is the basic idea behind thermal storage, but the deeper appeal is broader than one technology. It is the idea that buildings can become more intelligent participants in energy systems instead of passive recipients of whatever the grid throws at them.

This blog is where I will write through that idea in public.

## What I want to explore here

- How commercial cooling actually works in the field
- Why demand charges quietly reshape building economics
- Where thermal storage fits into existing HVAC systems
- How a portfolio of buildings can become dispatchable thermal load
- Why cooling may become one of the most important flexible resources on the grid

Some of these posts will be technical. Some will be commercial. Some will be closer to essays. But all of them will come back to the same question: how do we make cooling more efficient, more financially rational, and more useful to the grid?

That is the question behind Icebreaker, and it is the one I want this blog to answer over time.
