I am a mechanical and thermal engineer who has spent time thinking about heat in a lot of different contexts: defense-grade cooling systems, the hottest turbine sections of jet engines, and in the molten metal used for experimental nuclear fusion reactors. 

I've also spent a lot of time thinking about how to air conditioners work, because I love to be cool and comfortable. I mean, who doesn't?

I've always thought that the most interesting problems are often the ones hiding in plain sight.

Cooling is one of those problems.

![Founder of Icebreaker Energy](../images/nikko_profile_pic.jpg)

I started thinking more seriously about cooling after living through a muggy New York City summer in 2025 and noticing how hot our streets were. It felt like our city was straining to blast cold air into our offices and apartments, while simultaneously dumping enormous amounts of heat back into the city. In an already temperature record-setting summer, the streets got hotter, the sidewalks got smellier, and the air started feeling like a steam room. The city was sweating, and I was not a fan.

 Our buildings are gulping down enormous amounts of electricity to reject heat back into the city, right as the grid is already strained and electricity is at its most expensive. Comfort depends on cooling, but the way we deliver it often feels crude: make cold instantly, at the hottest and most expensive moment, and then deal with the pricy consequences.

To me, this seems like a systems-design failure.

Icebreaker Energy comes from the belief that cooling should be smarter. Buildings should be able to make cold when the grid is under less strain, store it, and use it later. That is the basic idea behind thermal storage, but the deeper appeal is broader than one technology. It is the idea that buildings can become more intelligent participants in energy systems instead of passive recipients of whatever the utility companies tell them.

This blog is where I will write through that idea in public.

## What I want to explore here

- How commercial cooling actually works in the field
- Where thermal storage fits into existing HVAC systems
- How a portfolio of buildings can become dispatchable thermal load (a thermal virtual power plant)
- Why cooling may become one of the most important flexible resources on the grid

The primary thesis I want to investigate answers one (admittedly multi-part) question: how do we make cooling more efficient, more financially viable, and more beneficial to the grid?
